The story in brief

How did a team with a third of the Yankees’ payroll win just as many games?

In 2002 the Oakland Athletics had the third-lowest payroll in baseball. They had just lost their stars Jason Giambi and Johnny Damon to richer clubs. They won 103 games anyway, exactly as many as the New York Yankees, who spent three times as much.

Michael Lewis told the story in the 2003 book Moneyball, and Brad Pitt played general manager Billy Beane in the 2011 film. The idea behind it was simple. Baseball was paying for the wrong statistics, so the skills that really win games were on sale.

This page checks that idea against the real numbers. It rebuilds the argument step by step from the season data, and it also looks at what the book and the film left out.

Nine chapters · about 15 minutes · every chart is interactive

1
First inning

An unfair game

In 2002 the New York Yankees spent $125.9 million on players. Oakland spent $40.0 million, 28th of 30 teams. Both won 103 games.

Money did matter that year. Across the league, payroll and wins had a correlation of 0.44. Rich teams bought wins on average, and the bottom left of the chart is crowded with cheap teams that lost. Oakland sits alone in the top left corner. The question Michael Lewis asked is the right one: how does a team end up there, and do it three years running?

Beane's A's could not win an auction for a star. So the job was to find wins the auction was pricing wrongly. That needs two things: a model of what actually produces wins, and proof that the market pays for something else. The next five innings build both.

2
Second inning

Set the target in runs

Paul DePodesta (Peter Brand in the film) started from the end. In the book he works out that a team needs about 95 wins to make the playoffs, that 95 wins takes outscoring opponents by about 135 runs, and that the 2002 roster should score 800 to 820 and allow 650 to 670.

The tool behind this is Bill James's Pythagorean expectation. Wins are hard to predict directly. Runs are not, and runs convert to wins at a steady rate.

Win% ≈ RS² / (RS² + RA²)

I checked it on 1,038 team seasons from 1962 to 2002. The formula predicts a team's final win total with a typical error of 4.0 wins. A plain linear version works just as well: every extra 9.6 runs of run differential is worth one win. Plug in DePodesta's +135 and you get 81 + 135 / 9.6 = 95.1 wins. His arithmetic holds exactly.

Run differential against wins, every team season 1990 to 2002
Wins scaled to a 162 game season. The 2002 A's and DePodesta's pre-season target are marked.
Dashed line: wins = 81 + run differential / 9.56, fitted on 1962 to 2001.

The actual 2002 A's scored 800 and allowed 654. Both inside the predicted range. That differential is worth 97 wins. They won 103, so about six wins came from sequencing and luck, including a 20 game streak with several one-run finishes.

3
Third inning

What actually makes runs

If the target is 800 runs, the next question is which hitting statistic best predicts runs. Baseball had paid for batting average for a century. Switch between the statistics below and watch how tightly each one tracks scoring.

Team statistic against runs scored per game, 1990 to 2002

Over the full 1962 to 2001 sample (1,008 team seasons, everything a front office could have known before 2002), the correlations with runs per game are: batting average .84, on-base percentage .90, slugging .93, OBP plus slugging .96, stolen bases .14. Stolen bases, the thing scouts called "speed", tell you almost nothing about scoring.

Put on-base and slugging in one regression and you get a working run model:

Runs per game = −4.82 + 16.03 × OBP + 10.16 × SLG   (R² = 0.93)

So a point of on-base percentage is worth about 1.6 times a point of slugging. In the book DePodesta argues for three times. My regression on public data does not get that high, but the direction is the same and it is the part the market missed: OBP counts for more than slugging, and both beat batting average.

4
Fourth inning

Outs are the currency

Why does on-base percentage matter so much? Because an inning ends after three outs and nothing else. OBP is simply the share of plate appearances that do not make an out. This is also why the A's almost never bunted or stole: both risk or spend outs.

The run expectancy table makes this concrete. It gives the average number of runs a team scored from each situation to the end of the inning, measured over every MLB game from 1999 to 2002. Click the bases and set the outs.

Run expectancy explorer
expected runs, rest of inning
Source: Tom Tango's run expectancy matrix, 1999 to 2002. League averages, so a weak hitter bunting loses a little less than shown.

With a runner on first and nobody out, a team expects 0.953 runs. A successful sacrifice bunt moves it to a runner on second with one out: 0.725. The "productive" play costs a quarter of a run. A steal of second has to succeed about 74% of the time just to break even. The 2002 A's stole 46 bases, the fewest in baseball, and drew 609 walks, sixth most.

5
Fifth inning

Replacing Giambi in the aggregate

After 2001 Oakland lost Jason Giambi (to the Yankees for seven years and $120 million), Johnny Damon and closer Jason Isringhausen. No single affordable player could replace Giambi's .477 on-base percentage. The move in the book and the film: stop trying. Replace the average of three lineup spots instead.

Three lineup spots, before and after
On-base percentage, from the season batting lines. Bars start at zero.
Jeremy Giambi was traded to Philadelphia for John Mabry in late May 2002 (Mabry: .322 OBP, .523 SLG in 211 PA). Justice's $7.0M is his listed salary; per the book the Yankees were covering half of it.

The three outgoing spots averaged a .364 OBP, exactly the figure quoted in the book. The three replacements averaged .380. Hatteberg, a catcher who could no longer throw, was taught first base and got on base at a .374 clip for $900,000. Giambi earned $10.4 million in New York that year.

The bullpen got the same treatment. Isringhausen's 34 saves left; Billy Koch arrived by trade and saved 44. Chad Bradford, the submarine pitcher nobody wanted because of how he looked, threw 75 games with a 3.11 ERA for $235,000.

The market had the price wrong

To test the pricing properly I ran the regression economists Jahn Hakes and Raymond Sauer made famous: explain each hitter's salary from his previous season's on-base percentage and slugging, controlling for playing time and contract status. The coefficient is what the market paid for each skill.

In four of the five seasons before the book, the market paid close to nothing for on-base percentage once slugging was accounted for, even though OBP is the more valuable skill. From 2003 to 2006 the price of OBP was three to four percent of salary per 10 points, every year. Hakes and Sauer's published study found the same correction and concluded the inefficiency was real and that it closed once the knowledge spread. Beane was buying a dollar for fifty cents, and then the sale ended.

Seventh inning

The season and the streak

The film's low point is real. On May 23 the A's were 20 and 26. From there they went 83 and 33, a .716 pace. The streak ran from August 13 to September 4: 20 straight wins, an American League record at the time, outscoring opponents 141 to 65.

One caution the numbers themselves offer. A 97 win team by run differential will occasionally win 20 in a row and finish with 103. The streak is the most cinematic part of the season and the least analytical. The method shows up in the 800 and the 654, which landed where DePodesta said they would.

8
Eighth inning

What the film left out

The movie gives you Hatteberg, Justice and Bradford. It barely mentions the players who produced most of the value. Oakland allowed the sixth fewest runs in baseball, and three young starters did most of that for almost no money.

2002 A's pitchers
2002 A's hitters by Runs Created
Bill James's basic formula: (H + BB) × TB / (AB + BB).
Tagged players are the bargain pickups featured in the book. Tejada was the AL MVP; Zito won the Cy Young Award.

Miguel Tejada and Eric Chavez created 220 runs between them for $5.75 million. Zito, Hudson and Mulder went 57 and 21 across 675 innings for $1.97 million combined. All five were developed by the club and were still years from free agency, so their pay was capped by the league's rules.

That is the honest reading of the data. The biggest inefficiency Oakland exploited was cheap pre-free-agency talent, much of it drafted and developed with traditional scouting. The OBP bargains were real, and they were the margin on top.

9
Ninth inning

Aftermath

Oakland lost the 2002 Division Series to Minnesota in five games. It was the third of four straight years they lost a first-round series three games to two. That looks like a flaw in the method until you do the arithmetic: a team that wins 55% of its games against a given opponent wins a best-of-five only 59% of the time. Short series are close to coin flips, and 162 games are not.

Cost per win: Oakland against the league average

From 2000 to 2003 the A's averaged 98 wins while ranking 23rd to 29th in payroll, paying about half the league rate per win. Then the edge was copied. Boston hired Bill James in November 2002, tried to hire Beane, hired Theo Epstein instead, and won the 2004 World Series with a large payroll and the same ideas. By 2007 Oakland was paying the league rate per win.

The lasting lesson is the procedure. Define the outcome (wins). Find what drives it (runs, then outs). Measure what the market pays for instead. Buy the difference, and expect the difference to close.

Five things to test

  1. Redo the third inning for the last ten seasons. Does OBP still beat batting average by the same margin now that strikeouts and home runs dominate?
  2. Find the 2002 teams that beat or missed their Pythagorean record by the most (Minnesota +7, Boston −8). Check their record in one-run games.
  3. Rebuild the run expectancy table for a recent season and recompute the steal break-even rate. The 2023 rule changes moved it.
  4. Look up the 2002 draft, the one in the book. Nick Swisher played 1,527 MLB games and Joe Blanton won 101. Jeremy Brown, the catcher the scouts laughed at, played 5.
  5. Pick any current low-payroll winner and ask what the market underprices today. Defense, pitch framing and pitch design have all had their turn.

The story in brief

Why did the mid-range jump shot almost disappear?

Twenty years ago more than a third of all NBA shots were mid-range jumpers, the shot Michael Jordan and Kobe Bryant built careers on. Today it is one shot in ten. Threes went the other way, from under a fifth of shots to over two fifths.

The change is often called Moreyball, after Daryl Morey, who ran the Houston Rockets from 2007 to 2020. He built a team around a piece of arithmetic. A three-pointer is worth 50% more than a two, and a long two goes in barely more often than a three.

This page follows that arithmetic through 4.4 million shots: where the league shoots from, what actually wins games, the team that went first, and what happened when everyone else copied it.

In 2003-04 the most common shot in basketball was the long two: 36% of all attempts came from mid-range. By 2024-25 it was 10%. Three-pointers went from 19% of shots to 42%. The rim held steady. Nobody changed the rules in between. Teams just did the arithmetic.

The arithmetic of a shot

A shot is a bet. Its value is the chance it goes in times the points it pays. Plot that by distance and the whole revolution is in one picture.

Accuracy falls fast in the first few feet and then goes nearly flat. In 2024-25 a 20 foot shot went in 37% of the time and a 24 foot shot, behind the line, 38%. So the step back costs no accuracy and adds 50% to the payout. The mid-range jumper is the worst bet on the floor, and the corner three, the shortest three there is, is one of the best.

4
The model

What actually wins games

Baseball had Bill James. Basketball had Dean Oliver, whose 2004 book Basketball on Paper did two things. It counted the game in possessions instead of minutes, so fast and slow teams could be compared. And it reduced winning to four factors: shooting, turnovers, rebounding and free throws.

eFG% = (FG + 0.5 × 3PM) / FGA    a made three counts as one and a half makes

Oliver's estimate of their importance was 40 / 25 / 20 / 15. I ran the regression on 1,314 team seasons from 1980 to 2026. The four factors, measured for a team and its opponents, explain 98.5% of the variation in point differential. The weights come out close to his, with shooting even more dominant.

The four factors: Oliver's weights against the regression
Regression weights are standardized coefficients for a team and its opponents combined, scaled to sum to 100.
Which team statistic tracks offense? 657 team seasons, 2004 to 2025

Shooting efficiency is most of offense. That is why shot selection matters so much: it is the cheapest way to raise eFG% without finding better shooters.

The Pythagorean idea carries over too. In baseball the best exponent was 1.85. In basketball it is 14.2, because a game has about 200 scoring chances and the better team almost always wins. Each point of average margin is worth 2.6 wins over 82 games, and the formula misses a team's win total by only 3.0 on average.

5
Houston Rockets, 2007 to 2020

The team that went first

Daryl Morey became Houston's general manager in 2007 with a background in statistics and consulting and no playing career. His Rockets took the shot chart to its logical end: threes, layups and free throws, and as close to nothing else as a team could manage. People called it Moreyball.

Mid-range share of shots: every team, every season
Each dot is a team. Houston is highlighted.
Houston reached a 15% mid-range share in 2012-13. The league average did not get there until 2018-19.

In one summer, between 2012 and 2013, Houston cut its mid-range share in half. By 2017-18 the Rockets took half their shots from three, won 65 games, and had the league's best record. In 2018-19 only 5.5% of their shots were mid-range jumpers. The rest of the league was six years behind in 2013 and has been closing the gap ever since.

6
The edge closes

The market corrects

Moneyball's edge lasted until everyone copied it. The same test works here. If shot selection is an edge, teams that take more threes should have better offenses than teams in the same season that take fewer. Here is that correlation, year by year.

Does shot diet still separate good offenses from bad ones?
Within-season correlation between a team's shot share and its offensive rating. Three-season rolling average.
Three-point shareMid-range share

In 2012-13 the signal peaked: teams that shot more threes had clearly better offenses (r = 0.60) and teams that lived in the mid-range had worse ones (r = −0.60). Since 2022 the three-point signal has faded to less than half its peak, and the mid-range penalty has disappeared and turned slightly positive. Every team has roughly the same shot chart now, so the chart no longer tells them apart. What separates teams again is who can make the shots.

League offense: points per 100 possessions

The strategy did not stop working. It became the baseline. League offense rose from 103 points per 100 possessions in 2004 to 116 in 2026, the most efficient basketball ever played, and three-point accuracy held at 36% even as volume more than doubled.

The catch

Living with variance

The three is the better bet on average. It is also the streakier one. Here is every team's three-point percentage in every single game of two seasons.

More attempts make each night more predictable: the spread has narrowed. But it is still wide. In 2024-25 one team game in ten finished under 26% from three, and one in ten over 46%. On 38 attempts that is a swing of more than 20 points from shooting luck alone.

That start position is the most famous night in this story. Houston led Golden State three games to two, then in Game 7 missed 27 threes in a row and finished 7 for 44. The model above says a night that cold happens about three times in a thousand games. It happened in the one that mattered. It is the same lesson as Oakland's playoff exits: a method that wins over 82 games can lose to a bad night.

And the mid-range is not dead. In 2024-25 teams that took more mid-range shots had slightly better offenses, not worse. Defenses now guard the rim and the line, and the players who can score from the space in between are valuable again. The edge moves to wherever the market is not looking.

Five things to test

  1. Pull one team's shot chart for this season and compute its expected points from league-average accuracy by zone. Is the offense good because of where it shoots or how well?
  2. Check the corner three. It is the shortest three and has the highest return. Which teams get the most of them, and who on the roster is creating them?
  3. Redo the fourth chapter for the playoffs only. Do the four factors keep the same weights when defenses tighten?
  4. Find the best mid-range shooters of the last three seasons. At what accuracy does a long two beat a league-average three? (The calculator above answers it: 54%.)
  5. Track free throw rate. It is the smallest of the four factors and the one rule changes move the most.

by the numbers

The story in brief

Leicester City won the Premier League at 5,000 to 1. Skill, or luck?

Soccer is a low-scoring game. A match has fewer than three goals on average, so one deflection can decide a result and a lucky run can decide a season. The league table mixes up the teams that played well with the teams that got the breaks.

Expected goals, or xG, was built to separate the two. It gives every shot a chance of scoring, based on where and how it was taken, and adds those chances up. A team that keeps creating good chances will usually win in the long run, even when the scoreboard says otherwise.

This page explains how xG works, tests whether it really predicts anything, and looks at the clubs that used it to beat richer rivals. It draws on 337,000 shots and 58,000 match results from Europe’s five big leagues.

Eight chapters · about 15 minutes · every chart is interactive

1
The unfair game, again

Money decides most of it

Start where Moneyball started. In the 2002 baseball season payroll and wins had a correlation of 0.44. European soccer has no salary cap, no draft and no playoffs to scramble the order, and it shows. Across 1,274 team seasons in the five big leagues, squad market value against points has a correlation of 0.83.

Of 65 league titles in this period, 61 went to one of the three most valuable squads. That is the wall a small club is up against. The dot far above the line is Leicester City in 2015-16: the eleventh most valuable squad in England, 31 points better than its budget predicts, and champions.

2
Why soccer is hard to measure

A game of rare events

A basketball game has about 200 scoring events. A soccer match has 2.75. When goals are that rare, they behave almost like random arrivals, and the distribution of goals per team per match sits very close to a Poisson curve.

This is the core problem. The team that takes more shots wins less than half the time. One deflection decides a match, and a run of them can decide a season. Points are a slow, noisy measure of how good a team is.

The on-base percentage of soccer

What expected goals is

If goals are too rare to trust, count something that happens more often and leads to them. A match has about 25 shots. Expected goals, or xG, gives each shot the probability that an average player scores from that position. Add them up and you have a measure of the chances a team created, with the luck of the finish removed.

The map below is that probability measured directly: 333,000 non-penalty shots from eight seasons, grouped into two metre squares. Hover or tap a square.

Location does most of the work. A shot from inside the six yard box scores one time in three. From the edge of the penalty area it is one in fourteen. From 25 metres it is about one in thirty-three. A full model adds more: the body part, the type of pass before it, whether it came from a set piece.

xG difference wins at every point in the season. The gap is largest early: after only six matches, xG difference predicts the rest of the season (r = 0.70) better than the league table does after nineteen (r = 0.68). A club that reads xG knows by October what the table will admit in January.

It works at match level too. The side with more shots won 48% of matches. The side with the higher xG won 60% and lost 15%. When the xG gap was a full goal or more, it won 77% and lost 6%.

Expected points

The table lies

Take every match, use the xG of each shot to work out the chance of each result, and award expected points. Add them up and you get the table that chance quality says a season deserved. Pick a league and a season.

Actual table against expected points
xPts: expected points from shot-by-shot xG. Diff: points minus xPts, colored when a team is 8 or more points above (orange) or below (green) expectation. xRank: position by expected points.

Try the Premier League in 2015-16. Leicester's 81 points came with 68.7 expected: a genuinely good team, fourth by chance quality, that also ran twelve points hot. Then try 2020-21 and find Brighton: 16th in the table, 5th by expected points. Two seasons later they finished sixth.

The difference between points and expected points is mostly luck, and luck does not carry over.

What the scouts overpay for

Finishing is mostly noise

Here is soccer's version of batting average. The transfer market pays for goals. But a striker's goals come from two things: how many good chances he gets (his xG) and how well he finishes them (goals minus xG). Only one of those is a stable skill.

One season against the next, 1,298 player pairs
Players with 1,500+ minutes and 30+ shots in both seasons. Per 90 minutes, penalties excluded.

Getting chances repeats almost perfectly from year to year (r = 0.83). Finishing above or below xG barely repeats at all (r = 0.03). A forward who scored 20 from 13 expected goals is far more likely to score 13 next year than 20. Next season's goals are better predicted by this season's xG (r = 0.75) than by this season's goals (r = 0.67).

So the Moneyball trade in soccer is: buy the player who gets chances and has been unlucky, sell the one on a hot streak. There are exceptions, and over eight seasons the data finds them. They are exactly who you would guess.

Soccer's three-point revolution, in reverse

Stop shooting from there

The NBA learned to shoot from further out. Soccer learned the opposite. A shot from outside the box scores 3.7% of the time. A shot inside it scores 14.3%. And a speculative shot ends the attack. Once clubs could see those numbers, players were coached to pass up the long shot and work the ball closer.

Share of shots taken from outside the penalty area
Non-penalty shots. The 2021-22 point covers the first half of that season.

It is a slower change than basketball's because there is no rule that pays a bonus at a fixed line. But the direction is the same in all five leagues: fewer hopeful efforts, better chances.

8
Who got there first

The clubs that read the numbers

Soccer's analytics pioneers did not come from baseball. They came from betting, where a better model of team strength is worth money every weekend.

  • Brentford and Midtjylland. Matthew Benham started in betting in 2001, founded the statistical research firm Smartodds in 2004, and became Brentford's majority owner in 2012. In 2014 he bought into Denmark's FC Midtjylland, who won their first league title the following season. Brentford reached the Premier League in 2021, their first top-flight season in 74 years, on one of the smallest budgets in the division.
  • Brighton. Tony Bloom, who had hired Benham two decades earlier, runs his own betting consultancy and has owned Brighton since 2009. The club's method is recruitment: buy undervalued players from overlooked leagues, sell them on at a profit, repeat.
  • Liverpool. This is the direct line to Moneyball. John Henry, the Red Sox owner who tried to hire Billy Beane in 2002, bought Liverpool in 2010. The club built a research department under physicist Ian Graham. By Graham's account the analysts pushed for Mohamed Salah in 2017 when the manager preferred another target. Liverpool won the Champions League in 2019 and the league in 2020.

Two honest limits. First, xG is not the whole truth. Atlético Madrid beat their expected points by 15 or more in four separate seasons and Juventus did it repeatedly. Teams that defend a lead deep, or have an elite goalkeeper, can beat a model that only sees where shots came from. Second, this edge is closing the way the others did. Every top club now has an analytics department, and the public can see xG on the broadcast.

The pattern across all three sports is the same. Find what actually produces winning, measure it better than the market does, act before the market catches up.

The formulas

xG of a shot = P(goal | location, body part, situation, ...)
Team xG = sum of the xG of its shots
P(k goals) = λ^k × e^−λ / k!    Poisson, with λ = expected goals
xPts = 3 × P(win) + 1 × P(draw), from each match's shots

Five things to test

  1. Open your own league's table and its xG table side by side after ten matches. Write down who is over-performing. Check again at the end of the season.
  2. Find a striker on a hot streak (goals well above xG) and one on a cold one with the same xG per 90. Compare their transfer values.
  3. Split xG into set pieces and open play. Some clubs, Brentford among them, have treated set pieces as an underpriced source of goals.
  4. Look at pressing. PPDA (opposition passes allowed per defensive action) measures how hard a team presses. Does it predict xG against?
  5. Redo chapter one for the latest season. Is money explaining more of the table now, or less?

A Nation of Cousins

Pick any two Ashkenazi Jews on earth and their DNA says they are about fourth or fifth cousins. This map shows how that happened: where the Jewish people lived, how many they were, and the few narrow doorways everyone alive today came through.

      Reading the map

      What the circles mean

      • Area is people. A circle four times the area holds four times as many Jews. Circles grow a little when you zoom so small communities stay visible.
      • Color is ancestry. Each color is one of the clusters that geneticists find when they compare Jewish communities. A circle with several colors is a place where they live side by side, as in Israel today.
      • Old numbers are soft. After 1880 the figures follow censuses and the standard demographic series. Before 1500 they are scholarly estimates with wide error bars, and ancient figures are disputed by a factor of two or more.
      • The timeline is stretched. The six years of the Shoah get as much room as six centuries of late antiquity, so playback slows down where the map changes fastest.
      The genetics

      The bottleneck up close

      In 2014 a team led by Shai Carmi sequenced 128 Ashkenazi genomes and found long stretches of DNA that are identical from person to person. Stretches that long only survive when a population has been very small very recently. Their model put the squeeze 25 to 32 generations back and its size at the genetic equivalent of about 350 people.

      The second half of the story is the rebound. From about 1500 the Jews of Poland-Lithuania doubled roughly every two generations for four centuries. A small founding group followed by fast growth and marriage inside the community is exactly the recipe that makes everyone a relative. It is also why a handful of rare mutations, such as those behind Tay-Sachs and Gaucher disease and several BRCA variants, became common in one population.

      Try it

      Why everyone is a cousin

      Your family tree doubles every generation: two parents, four grandparents, eight great-grandparents. Go back far enough and the tree needs more people than existed. The only way out is that the same ancestors fill many slots at once. Genealogists call this pedigree collapse. In a small, closed community it happens fast.

      This is why Columbia geneticist Itsik Pe'er could say that among Ashkenazi Jews “everyone is a 30th cousin.” On paper, any two share ancestors from the founding group. In the DNA, the overlap from all those shared ancestors adds up to about what two real fourth or fifth cousins share.

      The same holds inside the other communities. A 2010 study of seven groups, from Iranian and Iraqi to Italian, Greek, Turkish, Syrian and Ashkenazi Jews, found that members of each share DNA like fourth or fifth cousins, about ten times more than two strangers on a New York street. It also found that Ashkenazi and Sephardi Jews are closer to each other than their thousand years apart would suggest.

      Fact check

      Where the popular story slips

      The usual telling goes: the Black Death pogroms drove a few hundred survivors east to Poland, where clean hands and kosher kitchens let them multiply into millions. Parts of that hold up. Parts do not.

      The number

      350 is not a headcount

      It is an effective population size: the size of an idealized population that would leave the same genetic trace. Real populations are always larger than their effective size, often several times larger. Nobody counted 350 people walking to Poland.

      The timing

      It came before the plague

      In 2022 researchers read DNA from 33 people buried in the medieval Jewish cemetery of Erfurt. They already carried the founder signature: a third shared one maternal lineage and eight carried disease variants known in Ashkenazi Jews today. The founder event predates the 14th century.

      The mothers

      Four women, 40 percent

      Four maternal lineages account for about 40% of Ashkenazi Jews, not most of them. Where those four women came from is disputed. The 2006 study that found them argued for the Near East. A 2013 study argued for prehistoric Europe, which would point to local women joining the community.

      The cousins

      Fourth cousins in DNA only

      Two random Ashkenazi Jews are not literally fourth cousins. They share as much DNA as fourth or fifth cousins would, spread over many distant shared ancestors. The founders themselves were mixed, roughly half Middle Eastern and half European in ancestry.

      The growth

      Hygiene is a guess

      Hand-washing, ritual baths and quick burial are a popular explanation for the boom in Poland. There is no demographic record that tests it. Historians give at least equal weight to early marriage, communal welfare and a protected economic role.

      The ancient peak

      Five million is the high end

      The first-century figures on this map follow Botticini and Eckstein, who use 5 to 5.5 million. Archaeologists who estimate from settlement remains argue Roman Judea could not have fed 2.5 million people. If they are right, the ancient circles should be much smaller and the later shrinking less dramatic.

      Method

      How this was built

      The map holds 36 regions with fixed outlines. Poland, for example, always means its 1921 to 1939 borders, so its circle does not jump when frontiers move. Each region has a population figure at anchor years, split by community. Between anchors the page interpolates along a smooth growth curve.

      World totals from 1880 onward match the series published by Sergio DellaPergola and the American Jewish Year Book to within about 2%. Regional figures before 1800 are a reconstruction from standard reference estimates, and should be read as orders of magnitude. Converts out of Judaism, including the conversos of Spain and Portugal, are not counted after they convert. The split of Israel's population by origin is rough, because a large and growing share of Israelis have parents from different communities.

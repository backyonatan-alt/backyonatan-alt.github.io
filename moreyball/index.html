NBA, 2004 to 2026. A data investigation

Moreyball
by the numbers

Baseball's revolution was about who to pay. Basketball's was about where to stand. In twenty years the league moved its shots to two places on the floor, and the evidence is 4.4 million shot locations. This page rebuilds the argument from them.

In 2003-04 the most common shot in basketball was the long two: 36% of all attempts came from mid-range. By 2024-25 it was 10%. Three-pointers went from 19% of shots to 42%. The rim held steady. Nobody changed the rules in between. Teams just did the arithmetic.

Expected value

The arithmetic of a shot

A shot is a bet. Its value is the chance it goes in times the points it pays. Plot that by distance and the whole revolution is in one picture.

Points per shot by distance from the basket
League wide, every shot of the season. Hover for field goal percentage.
2024-252003-04
The jump at 22 to 24 feet is the three-point line (22 ft in the corners, 23.75 ft above the break).

Accuracy falls fast in the first few feet and then goes nearly flat. In 2024-25 a 20 foot shot went in 37% of the time and a 24 foot shot, behind the line, 38%. So the step back costs no accuracy and adds 50% to the payout. The mid-range jumper is the worst bet on the floor, and the corner three, the shortest three there is, is one of the best.

4
The model

What actually wins games

Baseball had Bill James. Basketball had Dean Oliver, whose 2004 book Basketball on Paper did two things. It counted the game in possessions instead of minutes, so fast and slow teams could be compared. And it reduced winning to four factors: shooting, turnovers, rebounding and free throws.

eFG% = (FG + 0.5 × 3PM) / FGA    a made three counts as one and a half makes

Oliver's estimate of their importance was 40 / 25 / 20 / 15. I ran the regression on 1,314 team seasons from 1980 to 2026. The four factors, measured for a team and its opponents, explain 98.5% of the variation in point differential. The weights come out close to his, with shooting even more dominant.

The four factors: Oliver's weights against the regression
Regression weights are standardized coefficients for a team and its opponents combined, scaled to sum to 100.
Which team statistic tracks offense? 657 team seasons, 2004 to 2025

Shooting efficiency is most of offense. That is why shot selection matters so much: it is the cheapest way to raise eFG% without finding better shooters.

The Pythagorean idea carries over too. In baseball the best exponent was 1.85. In basketball it is 14.2, because a game has about 200 scoring chances and the better team almost always wins. Each point of average margin is worth 2.6 wins over 82 games, and the formula misses a team's win total by only 3.0 on average.

5
Houston Rockets, 2007 to 2020

The team that went first

Daryl Morey became Houston's general manager in 2007 with a background in statistics and consulting and no playing career. His Rockets took the shot chart to its logical end: threes, layups and free throws, and as close to nothing else as a team could manage. People called it Moreyball.

Mid-range share of shots: every team, every season
Each dot is a team. Houston is highlighted.
Houston reached a 15% mid-range share in 2012-13. The league average did not get there until 2018-19.
Houston, season by season

In one summer, between 2012 and 2013, Houston cut its mid-range share in half. By 2017-18 the Rockets took half their shots from three, won 65 games, and had the league's best record. In 2018-19 only 5.5% of their shots were mid-range jumpers. The rest of the league was six years behind in 2013 and has been closing the gap ever since.

The edge closes

The market corrects

Moneyball's edge lasted until everyone copied it. The same test works here. If shot selection is an edge, teams that take more threes should have better offenses than teams in the same season that take fewer. Here is that correlation, year by year.

Does shot diet still separate good offenses from bad ones?
Within-season correlation between a team's shot share and its offensive rating. Three-season rolling average.
Three-point shareMid-range share

In 2012-13 the signal peaked: teams that shot more threes had clearly better offenses (r = 0.60) and teams that lived in the mid-range had worse ones (r = −0.60). Since 2022 the three-point signal has faded to less than half its peak, and the mid-range penalty has disappeared and turned slightly positive. Every team has roughly the same shot chart now, so the chart no longer tells them apart. What separates teams again is who can make the shots.

League offense: points per 100 possessions

The strategy did not stop working. It became the baseline. League offense rose from 103 points per 100 possessions in 2004 to 116 in 2026, the most efficient basketball ever played, and three-point accuracy held at 36% even as volume more than doubled.

Until 2015 no league leader had taken nine threes a night. Then Stephen Curry took 11.2 in 2015-16 and made 45% of them, and James Harden took 13.2 in 2018-19 for Houston. The ceiling moved by half in four seasons.

The catch

Living with variance

The three is the better bet on average. It is also the streakier one. Here is every team's three-point percentage in every single game of two seasons.

Single-game three-point percentage, every team game
2003-04 (16 attempts a game)2024-25 (38 attempts a game)
Share of team games in each 2.5 point band. Games with at least 10 attempts.

More attempts make each night more predictable: the spread has narrowed. But it is still wide. In 2024-25 one team game in ten finished under 26% from three, and one in ten over 46%. On 38 attempts that is a swing of more than 20 points from shooting luck alone.

Try it: how often does a good shooting team go cold?
Binomial model: each shot independent. Start position is Houston in Game 7 of the 2018 conference finals: 44 attempts by a 36% team.

That start position is the most famous night in this story. Houston led Golden State three games to two, then in Game 7 missed 27 threes in a row and finished 7 for 44. The model above says a night that cold happens about three times in a thousand games. It happened in the one that mattered. It is the same lesson as Oakland's playoff exits: a method that wins over 82 games can lose to a bad night.

And the mid-range is not dead. In 2024-25 teams that took more mid-range shots had slightly better offenses, not worse. Defenses now guard the rim and the line, and the players who can score from the space in between are valuable again. The edge moves to wherever the market is not looking.

Overtime

Dig in yourself

The formulas

Points per shot = FG% × shot value
eFG% = (FG + 0.5 × 3PM) / FGA
TS% = PTS / (2 × (FGA + 0.44 × FTA))
Possessions ≈ FGA − ORB + TOV + 0.44 × FTA
Offensive rating = 100 × PTS / possessions

Five things to test

  1. Pull one team's shot chart for this season and compute its expected points from league-average accuracy by zone. Is the offense good because of where it shoots or how well?
  2. Check the corner three. It is the shortest three and has the highest return. Which teams get the most of them, and who on the roster is creating them?
  3. Redo the fourth chapter for the playoffs only. Do the four factors keep the same weights when defenses tighten?
  4. Find the best mid-range shooters of the last three seasons. At what accuracy does a long two beat a league-average three? (The calculator above answers it: 54%.)
  5. Track free throw rate. It is the smallest of the four factors and the one rule changes move the most.
